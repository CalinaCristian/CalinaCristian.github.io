## What stands there and who made it
- **Today's pole is the 1940 replica.** In 1938 an arsonist damaged the 1899 pole. It was taken down on 12 April 1939 and found to be beyond repair (fire and dry rot). The U.S. Forest Service, which was running a totem-pole restoration programme in southeast Alaska, had a replica carved by **Tlingit carvers employed through the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC)**. The work was done at **Saxman, Alaska**, directed by Tlingit carver **Charles Brown**, and the team **included members of the Kinninook family**, the pole's original owners (Jonaitis 2017 p. 6; Garfield 1996 p. 13).
  - It took about three months to carve.
  - The cedar came from Forest Service land and the carvers were paid by the government, so an act of Congress was needed to transfer the pole to the City of Seattle.
  - It was dedicated with tribal blessings, shipped to Seattle in April 1940 and **raised on 24 July 1940**.
- **Material and size.** The replica is about **50 ft (15.2 m)** tall and 4.5 ft wide. It was carved from a 70 ft **western red cedar** from Kina Cove near Kasaan, Alaska. The original was 49 ft 8 in tall and carved from **hemlock**.
- **Restoration.** In **1972** the pole was restored and repainted by **Tsimshian** carver **John C. Hudson Jr.** (Seattle Department of Neighborhoods).
- **Status.** Designated a **National Historic Landmark in 1977**, together with the Pioneer Building and the pergola ("Pioneer Building, Pergola, and Totem Pole", NRHP 77001340).
- **Other carvers.** The names of the CCC carvers other than Charles Brown are **not in the sources reachable from here**. Garfield's book is the place to confirm them. Do not invent names.

## The original pole (c. 1790 to 1938)
- It is called the **Chief-of-All-Women pole**. It was carved around 1790 for the **Kinninook family, a Tlingit clan of the Raven moiety**, to honour a Tlingit woman known as Chief-of-All-Women. She drowned in the Nass River while travelling to visit an ill sister.
  - Her family hired a carver and told him the stories they wanted shown.
  - They raised the pole at a potlatch in the Tlingit village on **Tongass Island, Alaska**.
  - It was one of the few poles dedicated to a woman.
- **Taken in 1899.** On 28 August 1899 the Seattle Post-Intelligencer's "Business Men's" goodwill expedition, on the steamship *City of Seattle*, landed at Fort Tongass while the villagers were away at the fishing and cannery season.
  - Third mate R. D. McGillvery and sailors cut the pole down and sawed it in two. They broke the beak of the bottom figure, which was later reconstructed incorrectly.
  - The pole was unveiled in Pioneer Square on **18 October 1899** as a "gift" to the city.
- **The Tlingit response.**
  - William E. Kinninook claimed $10,000 from the city; the claim was refused.
  - A federal grand jury in Juneau indicted eight expedition members for theft of government property.
  - The Post-Intelligencer paid $500 each to four claimants ($2,000).
  - The case was dismissed in June 1900. A further $500 settlement followed: $300 for the pole and $200 for a monument.
- **Repainting.** The original had been repainted with successive coats of **non-Tlingit colours**. The replica returned to the **native Tlingit colours: black, red and blue-green** (Garfield p. 15).

## Figures, top to bottom (Garfield p. 17)
A pole is read from the top; the top figure identifies the owner.

| # | Figure | Story it belongs to | Modelling notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (top) | **Raven**, with the **crescent moon in his beak** | *Raven Steals the Sun, Stars and Moon* | Projecting beak with the moon crescent; folded wings in formline |
| 2 | **Woman holding her frog child** | *The Woman Who Married the Frog* | Human face with the small frog held in front (arms/hands carved in relief) |
| 3 | **The woman's frog husband** | same | Wide frog mouth, no ears, squat limbs |
| 4 | **Mink** | *Raven and Mink inside the Whale* | Smaller animal figure, long body |
| 5 | **Raven** (second) | same | Beak (do not confuse with the top Raven) |
| 6 | **Whale with a seal in his mouth** | same | Blowhole, fin, the seal held in the jaws |
| 7 (bottom) | **Raven-at-the-Head-of-Nass**, also called **Grandfather of Raven** | *Raven Steals the Sun* (the chief who owned the sun, moon and stars) | Large human-like face at the base. The original's beak was broken in 1899 and wrongly restored |

The three stories, in short (Garfield pp. 23–30, Sherwood 1975):
- **Raven Steals the Sun.** Raven becomes a needle in the chief's daughter's cup, is born as the chief's grandson, gets the moon and stars, then flies off through the smoke hole with the box holding the sun.
- **The Frog Husband.** A woman insults the frogs and is taken as wife by a frog-man under a lake. Her father drains the lake.
- **Raven and Mink in the Whale.** They light a fire inside the whale, eat its fat, cut out its heart, and are freed when the whale washes ashore. Raven comes out glossy; Mink comes out brown from drying in rotten wood.

## Colours and surface (for the model)
- Paint is **black (formline), red (secondary formline / lips / nostrils) and blue-green (eye sockets, tertiary areas)** on the **unpainted grey-brown weathered cedar** body. No other colours; no "blue blocks".
- Deep relief, with beaks, fins, wings and the frog child projecting from the column. It is **not** a smooth cylinder with flat faces.
- Before modelling, check proportions and exact paint placement against the Commons category "Pioneer Square totem pole" (reference only). Owners/guides speak about it respectfully (seattle-spots.md §culture).

## Suggested plaque text (factual, credits first)
> **Chief-of-All-Women pole (replica).** Carved 1939–40 at Saxman, Alaska, by Tlingit carvers led by Charles Brown, among them members of the Kinninook family, for whom the original was carved about 1790 at Tongass Island to honour Chief-of-All-Women. The original was taken without consent by a Seattle business excursion in 1899 and burned in 1938. Restored in 1972 by Tsimshian carver John C. Hudson Jr. National Historic Landmark (1977). Figures, top to bottom: Raven with the moon; a woman holding her frog child; her frog husband; Mink; Raven; Whale holding a seal; Raven-at-the-Head-of-Nass.

Wording note: say "taken without consent" or "stolen" (the article's words). Do not present it as a gift. Context worth knowing: the Coast Salish peoples native to Seattle did not traditionally carve totem poles. The city reviewed its totem poles for cultural sensitivity in 2018.

## Surroundings of Pioneer Place (same fidelity pass)
- **Pergola**: cast iron and glass, Victorian, triangular.
  - Built in **1909** by architect **Julian F. Everett** as a cable-car shelter over an underground comfort station.
  - About **60 ft long and 16 ft high** on iron columns, with ornate ironwork and a glass roof.
  - Restored 1972–73 (James E. Casey / UPS donation). Destroyed by a semi-truck on 15 January 2001 and **rebuilt by Seidelhuber Iron and Bronze Works, reopened 17 August 2002**. It is now protected by bollards.
  - Source: Wikipedia "Pioneer Square pergola".
- **Chief Seattle bust**: bronze by **James A. Wehn, 1909**, on a former drinking fountain for people, dogs and horses (made for the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exposition). It stands at 1st Ave × Yesler Way. Source: Wikipedia "Bust of Chief Seattle".
- **Pioneer Building**: 1892, architect **Elmer H. Fisher**, Richardsonian Romanesque. Stone, red brick, terracotta and cast iron. NE corner of 1st Ave × James St, on the site of Henry Yesler's house. Source: Wikipedia "Pioneer Building (Seattle)".
